This manuscript is the source for the bundled starter curriculum. `node scripts/build-curriculum.mjs`
compiles it into `backend/src/QuinntyneBrownStewardship.Cli/Content/starter-curriculum.json`, which an
operator imports with the `import-curriculum` verb. Nothing here is read by the running application.

It teaches the redemptive framework published by [FaithTech](https://faithtech.com/): the 4D Cycle of
Discover, Discern, Develop and Demonstrate, preceded by Prepare, with the Co-Creation Cycle inside
Develop. Stage definitions, the movements of lament, the postures of discernment, the Co-Creation
Cycle and the Scripture selections are taken from [The FaithTech Playbook](https://faithtech.com/playbook)
and the [FaithTech Workbook](https://github.com/FaithTechCreate/workbook), used and adapted under
[CC BY 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/4.0/). The teaching prose around them is this
repository's own. FaithTech does not endorse this software.

# 01 Prepare to build together

Take up the posture redemptive work asks for, and gather the problem, the people and the time before the cycle begins.

## Three ways to build

Most technology is built in one of two postures. The first moves fast and breaks things, treating speed as the highest good and damage as the cost of progress. The second slows down and fixes things, adding review and restraint until harm is less likely. The second is better than the first. Neither is what this programme is for.

FaithTech names a third posture: redemptive technology, which sacrifices and creates things. It does not merely avoid harm; it accepts cost in order to repair. The FaithTech Playbook puts the conviction plainly — there is a way of building technology that redemptively changes the world while transforming those who build it.

Write down a decision you have made in your own work. Ask which of the three postures it came from. Most of us can find all three in a single week, so the useful question is not which one you are, but which one you were in when it mattered.

## What redemptive impact means

Ask an ordinary product team to measure impact and you will get counts: users, sessions, revenue, time saved. Those numbers are real, and they are not the measure this cycle uses. FaithTech measures redemptive impact by the relationships that develop — with God, with the team, and with the people the work is meant to serve. The framing is credited to Andy Crouch.

That measure is slower and harder to fake. A count can rise while every relationship around the work decays. A relationship cannot deepen without someone actually being served. Choosing the harder measure early keeps the rest of the cycle honest, because it decides in advance what success will be allowed to mean.

Before you begin, name the three relationships this work will touch. Say what each looks like now, in one sentence, without flattering yourself. You will return to these at the end of the cycle and ask whether they went anywhere.

## Sourcing a problem worth the work

A cycle is only as good as the problem it starts from, and good problems are found rather than invented. They come from listening: to what breaks people's hearts for their community, to the needs a local non-profit or church already carries, to what you notice walking through a neighbourhood with your eyes open.

Resist the problem that arrives already shaped like a solution. If a request names the technology before it names the person, you have been handed someone's answer, not their difficulty. Ask what happens to a real person today, and keep asking until you can describe the difficulty without mentioning software.

Bring three candidate problems to your team. For each, name a person affected and how you could learn more from them this month. Discard any problem where that question has no answer, however interesting the problem is.

## Forming a team

Redemptive work is done in company. A team assembles the skills the work needs, but it also assembles the people who will hold each other to the posture when the deadline presses. That second function is not softer than the first; it is what keeps the cycle from collapsing back into moving fast and breaking things.

Balance matters more than seniority. A team of only engineers will build well and understand narrowly. Include someone who will spend time with the people affected, someone who will ask the awkward question, and someone who will keep the work honest about what it has not yet proven.

Agree roles before the pressure arrives, and write them down. Say who decides when the team cannot agree, who speaks with the people you are serving, and who is responsible for stopping the work if it stops serving them.

## Setting a timeline you can keep

The cycle runs on a rhythm: a stage, a pause, a conversation, then the next stage. That rhythm is the point. A team that sprints through Discover to reach Develop has not saved time; it has simply moved the discovery into the code, where it costs more to change.

Give each stage a real interval and defend it. Two weeks per stage suits most teams, with a conversation at the end of each. Put the dates in a calendar before you start, because a schedule agreed under enthusiasm is easier to keep than one negotiated under pressure.

Decide now what you will do when the timeline slips, as it will. The redemptive answer is usually to narrow the work rather than to shorten the listening. Write that commitment down while it is still cheap to make.

## Practice · 45–60 minutes

1. Write one paragraph describing a recent decision in your own work, and name which of the three postures — reckless, responsible or redemptive — it came from. Be specific about what it cost and who paid.
2. Bring three candidate problems, each described without naming any technology. For each, name one person affected and one way you could learn from them within a month.
3. Name the three relationships this cycle will touch, describe the current state of each in a sentence, and agree with your team who holds which role, including who may stop the work.

## Prepare

- Which posture did your last significant decision actually come from?
- Whose difficulty are you closest to, and how do you know it is theirs rather than your reading of it?
- What would have to be true at the end of this cycle for you to say a relationship had deepened?

# 02 Discover through lament

Reorient yourself to see, through the lens of Christ, those most affected by the problem, and position yourself humbly through a practice of lament.

## Reorienting before solving

Builders run to solutions. It is the instinct the work rewards, and in the Discover stage it is the instinct to interrupt. FaithTech describes this stage as reorienting ourselves to see, through the lens of Christ, those most affected by the problem — and it presses pause on the tendency to solve before that seeing has happened.

Reorienting is not a delay you tolerate before the real work. What you see in this stage determines what you are able to build later. A team that skips it does not build faster; it builds confidently in the wrong direction and discovers the error when changing course is expensive.

For the length of this stage, hold a rule: no proposed solution goes in the document. Collect difficulties, people and observations. When a solution arrives unbidden, write it on a separate page and leave it there until Discern.

## Who is most affected

Every problem has people at its centre and people at its edge, and the difference matters. The person most affected is rarely the person who reports the problem, and almost never the person who commissions the work. They are more often the person with the least room to route around it.

Name them concretely. "Users" is not an answer; neither is "the elderly" or "the unbanked". Name a person you could actually meet, describe their day, and say what this difficulty costs them in time, money, dignity or opportunity. If you cannot, you have not discovered the problem yet.

List at least three groups of people the problem touches, then rank them by how little choice they have. Spend your remaining time in this stage with the group at the top of that list.

## The practice of lament

In the FaithTech community a problem is commonly expressed through lament. Lament is not complaint and it is not despair. It is the practice of bringing a real grief to God honestly, without rushing to resolve it, and it is how this framework keeps a problem from becoming an abstraction.

Lament changes the person who prays it. It puts the difficulty in front of God rather than in front of a backlog, and it refuses the comfort of an immediate fix. A team that has lamented a problem together carries it differently into the stages that follow.

Nehemiah gives the pattern. Hearing that the walls of Jerusalem lay in ruins, he did not first draw a plan: "Lord, the God of heaven, the great and awesome God, who keeps his covenant of love with those who love him and keep his commandments, let your ear be attentive and your eyes open to hear the prayer your servant is praying before you day and night."

## Six movements of a lament

The FaithTech Workbook gives lament six movements, and they are worth following in order. Address God. Describe the problem to Him. Recall testimony of God's faithfulness. Petition Him. Ask for God's intervention. Praise Him in advance.

The order does work. Describing the problem to God, rather than about Him, keeps the account truthful. Recalling His faithfulness before petitioning keeps the request from becoming a demand. Praising in advance is not pretending the problem is solved; it is refusing to let the problem have the last word.

Write the lament as a team. Talk it through and have one person keep the pen. It will feel slow, and it will be the part of this stage you remember when the work gets hard.

## Writing the problem down

A discovered problem should be sayable in two or three sentences by anyone on the team, in the same way. Until it is, you do not have one problem; you have several, held privately, that will surface as disagreement later.

Write it without a solution in it. "People cannot book a room" is a problem. "There is no booking app" is a missing solution wearing a problem's clothes. The difference decides how much room Discern will have to work in.

Read your statement back to someone affected by it, and ask what it gets wrong. Their correction is the most valuable thing you will collect in this stage. Change the statement to match what they tell you, not what you meant.

## Practice · 60–75 minutes

1. List at least three groups of people the problem affects, then rank them by how little choice they have about living with it. Describe the group at the top in enough detail that a stranger could recognise one of them.
2. Write a lament as a team, following the six movements in order: address God, describe the problem to Him, recall His faithfulness, petition Him, ask for His intervention, and praise Him in advance.
3. Write the problem in two or three sentences with no solution in them. Read it to someone affected, ask what it gets wrong, and revise it to match their correction.

## Prepare

- Who is most affected by this problem, and how much choice do they have about it?
- What did writing the lament change about how you hold the problem?
- Which part of your problem statement did the person affected correct?

# 03 Discern the wisdom of God

Involve the wisdom of God, testing and refining your approach continually, and move from human-centred thinking to God-centred wisdom.

## Beyond our own frames of reference

Discernment in this framework is not a better brainstorm. The Playbook puts it this way: true discernment often comes from beyond our own frames of reference, and we seek to uncover and understand the will of God. That is a claim about where good answers come from, and it changes how the stage is run.

A team that believes the answer is already inside the room optimises for confidence and speed. A team that believes the answer may come from outside it optimises for listening — to God, to the people affected, and to the members whose instinct runs against the majority.

Paul names the shift: "Do not conform to the pattern of this world, but be transformed by the renewing of your mind. Then you will be able to test and approve what God's will is—his good, pleasing and perfect will."

## How might Jesus

Design practice asks "how might we". This framework asks a different question: how might Jesus. Take the lament and the problem statement from Discover and reframe them as that question. How might Jesus address social media addiction. How might Jesus address a food bank's waiting list.

The reframing is not decoration. "How might we" quietly bounds the answer to what this team can build with the skills and budget it has. "How might Jesus" removes that bound, and the ideas that arrive are noticeably less shaped by what is convenient for the builders.

Brainstorm at least ten approaches against that question. Do not filter as you go. The tenth idea is often the one that could not have arrived while you were still being sensible.

## Reject, Receive, Reimagine, Create

Not every good idea should be built by you, and the framework makes that a deliberate judgement rather than an afterthought. Sort each idea into one of four postures. Reject: the solution is not the answer. Receive: an existing solution can be used. Reimagine: an existing solution can be re-purposed. Create: something new needs to be built.

Most teams reach for Create because it is the posture that feels like work. It is usually the wrong one. Receiving something that already exists and serves people well is a better outcome than building a second thing that serves them adequately, and it leaves your team free for the problem nobody has taken.

Go through your ten ideas and give each a posture, with a sentence of reasoning. Notice which posture you gravitated to, and ask honestly whether the reasoning followed the evidence or the preference.

## Choosing by redemptive impact

When several ideas survive, choose between them by redemptive impact rather than by reach or by ease. Ask which one deepens relationship — with God, within the team, and with the people served. Ask which one you could still defend if it only ever reached a dozen people.

Reach is seductive because it is measurable early. Depth is not, and a decision made on reach alone will quietly select for the option that serves many people shallowly over the one that serves a few people properly. Both can be right; the framework insists you choose knowingly.

Write the chosen approach beside the one you rejected most reluctantly, with the reason. You will want that record in Develop, when the chosen path is difficult and the rejected one starts to look easy.

## Holding the decision loosely

Discern does not end when a decision is made. The stage's own definition is testing and refining the approach continually, which means the decision is a working hypothesis carried into Develop rather than a commitment defended there.

Say in advance what would change your mind. A team that names its disconfirming evidence before it starts building can recognise that evidence later; a team that does not will explain it away, because by then the decision has become an identity.

Write one sentence: if we learn this, we will stop or change course. Keep it where the team will see it during Develop. Revisiting it is not failure; it is the stage doing what it was designed to do.

## Practice · 60–75 minutes

1. Reframe your lament and problem statement as a "How might Jesus" question, then brainstorm at least ten approaches without filtering as you go.
2. Sort every idea into Reject, Receive, Reimagine or Create, with a sentence of reasoning for each. Note which posture you reached for most, and why.
3. Choose one approach on redemptive impact rather than reach, record the approach you rejected most reluctantly with your reason, and write the single sentence that would make you change course.

## Prepare

- Which posture did you gravitate to, and did the reasoning follow the evidence or the preference?
- How did "how might Jesus" change what you were willing to consider?
- What would have to be true for you to abandon the approach you chose?

# 04 Develop with the Holy Spirit

Recognise that God establishes your steps to develop the solution, and co-develop with the Holy Spirit through the Co-Creation Cycle.

## God establishes our steps

The Develop stage carries a claim that sits oddly beside a sprint board: what we need already exists in God, and through the Holy Spirit He will reveal it to us, so that what is not yet visible can be brought into view. Building, in this framework, is less invention than reception.

That does not make planning unspiritual or craft unnecessary. It changes who is understood to be leading. A team co-developing with the Holy Spirit still writes tests, still refactors, still ships — but it holds its plan open, and it expects the shape of the work to be corrected while it is being built.

Hebrews states the ground of it: "By faith we understand that the universe was formed at God's command, so that what is seen was not made out of what was visible." What is not yet visible is not therefore absent.

## The Co-Creation Cycle

FaithTech gives this stage a practice with five movements — Request, Receive, Review, Render, Rejoice — run as a cycle rather than a checklist. One iteration might take an hour or a fortnight. The point is that it repeats, and that building sits inside it rather than beside it.

The five movements are easy to name and hard to keep. Under deadline a team will skip Receive because it is silent, skip Review because it feels like talking, and skip Rejoice because the next thing is already due. What remains is Request and Render — a prayer at the start and a lot of code, which is roughly where most Christian teams already are.

Run one full iteration on the smallest useful piece of work before you attempt it on the whole. The practice is learned at small scale and then trusted at large scale, not the other way round.

## Request and Receive

Request is an invitation, not a formality. Write a prayer that names this specific piece of work and asks the Holy Spirit into it. Specificity matters: a team that prays about its actual design problem is listening for an actual answer.

Receive is the movement teams find hardest, because it asks for silence. Spend ten minutes not talking and not typing, and note what comes — thoughts, images, a passage of Scripture, a discomfort about a decision already made. Write them down without evaluating them; evaluation is the next movement's job.

Do both as a team, in the same room, at the same time. Received individually and reported later, these become anecdotes. Received together, they become something the team can weigh together.

## Review and Render

Review brings what was received into one place and weighs it. Some of it will be obviously useful, some obviously not, and some will be uncomfortable in a way that deserves attention rather than dismissal. Look for the note that more than one person received independently.

Render is where the work gets built. Outline the next steps the review points towards, then do them with the full seriousness of ordinary engineering: tests, review, honest naming, small reversible changes. Nothing in this framework excuses careless work; the co-creation claim raises the standard rather than lowering it.

Keep Render bounded by what Review actually supported. When a step in the plan turns out to have no line back to anything received or observed, that is worth noticing before it is built rather than after.

## Rejoice

Rejoice closes the iteration by thanking God for what came of it. It is the movement most easily cut, and cutting it changes the team. A team that never stops to name what was given comes to experience the work as entirely its own achievement, which is the posture the whole framework exists to interrupt.

It need not be long. A short prayer of thanks at the end of an iteration, naming something specific that was received rather than produced, is enough. Specific is the operative word: gratitude in general costs nothing and forms no one.

Then begin again. The cycle turns for as long as the work does, and the discipline compounds — not because each iteration is dramatic, but because a team that has run it fifty times has become a different kind of team.

## Practice · 60–90 minutes

1. Choose the smallest useful piece of your chosen approach and run one full iteration of the Co-Creation Cycle as a team: write a prayer inviting the Holy Spirit into the work, then spend ten minutes in silence noting what comes.
2. Review what was received, summarise the insights in one place, and mark anything more than one person received independently. Then outline the next steps those insights point towards and build them.
3. Close the iteration with a short prayer of thanks naming one specific thing that was received rather than produced, and record how this iteration differed from your usual way of working.

## Prepare

- What came during the silence that you would not have arrived at by discussion?
- Which of the five movements did you find hardest to keep, and what pressure made it hard?
- Where did the work you rendered go beyond what the review actually supported?

# 05 Demonstrate impact that lasts

Redefine impact as friendship compounded by time, measure and demonstrate it redemptively, and return to the cycle.

## Friendship compounded by time

The final stage asks what the work amounted to, and answers with a definition most technology organisations would reject: impact is friendship compounded by time. Not reach in the first quarter, not adoption, not force applied quickly — depth, accumulated slowly, between people who stayed.

The definition is inconvenient on purpose. It cannot be reported at the end of a two-week sprint, and it does not photograph well. What it does is name the thing that was actually at stake for the person you met in Discover, who did not need a product so much as they needed someone to stay with the difficulty.

Return to the three relationships you named in Prepare. Describe where each one stands now, in the same plain sentences. The comparison is your first honest measure of this cycle.

## Measuring what deepens

You still need measures, and they can be built. Ask about the depth of relationships formed: who knows whom now who did not before, and who would answer the phone. Ask about the spiritual growth of the team and of the people served. Ask what changed in the wider community, and over what period.

Write the measures down before you gather them, or you will find the ones that flatter the work. For each, say what number or observation would count as no progress. A measure that cannot come back negative is not a measure; it is an advertisement.

Pair every count you keep with a relationship it is meant to stand for. When the count rises and the relationship has not, you have learned something more useful than the count.

## Telling the story honestly

Demonstrating means telling people what happened, and the temptation is to tell it as a success. Tell it instead as it was: what you set out to do, what you learned in Discover that changed it, what you rejected in Discern, and what did not work in Develop.

An honest account is more useful to the next team than a triumphant one, and it is a truer offering. Share it where it can be examined — at a community gathering, with the people you served, with the partner who brought you the problem. Invite the correction you invited in Discover.

Include what you would not do again. That sentence is usually the most valuable thing a team produces, and it is almost always the first thing cut from the retrospective.

## Returning to the cycle

The 4D Cycle is a cycle. Demonstrate does not close the work; it hands it back to Discover with better sight. What you learned about the people most affected is now the starting material for the next turn, and the problem you can see this time was invisible before.

Decide deliberately what happens next. Continue, hand the work to someone better placed, or stop. Stopping well is a legitimate outcome and a rare skill, and a team that has kept its relationships can stop without abandoning anyone.

Whatever you decide, say it to the people affected before you say it anywhere else. They carried the difficulty that started this, and they should not learn the ending from an announcement.

## Practice · 45–75 minutes

1. Return to the three relationships you named in Prepare, describe where each stands now in plain sentences, and set the two accounts side by side.
2. Write three measures of redemptive impact covering depth of relationship, spiritual growth and change in the community. For each, state in advance what would count as no progress.
3. Tell the story of the cycle to someone outside the team, including what you rejected, what did not work, and what you would not do again. Then decide with the team whether to continue, hand the work on, or stop.

## Prepare

- Which relationship went somewhere, and what actually moved it?
- Which of your measures could never have come back negative?
- What would you not do again, and who else needs to hear it?
